Stair stringers makes your staircase safe and sturdy. Cutting them requires precision, careful planning, and the right tools to ensure your stairs meet building codes and provide reliable support. We’ll walk you through the entire process, from basic concepts to advanced techniques. What Are Stair Stringers?

Stair stringers are the diagonal supports that run along the sides of a staircase, providing the framework for treads and risers. Typically cut from 2-by-12 lumber, stringers are notched to create a zigzag pattern that accommodates the steps. They transfer the load of the stairs and people using them to the upper and lower levels of the structure. Importance of Proper Stringer Cutting

Accurately cut stringers are crucial for several reasons:

Safety: Properly cut stringers ensure even step heights and depths, reducing the risk of trips and falls.

Structural integrity: Well-cut stringers distribute weight evenly and provide adequate support for the entire staircase.

Code compliance: Stringers must meet local building codes for riser height, tread depth, and overall staircase angle.

Longevity: Correctly cut stringers are less likely to warp, crack, or fail over time. iStock

Tools Needed for Cutting Stair Stringers

Here’s a breakdown of the essential equipment you’ll need for cutting stair stringers. Measuring Tools

Accurate measurements are the foundation of well-cut stringers. Key measuring tools include the following:

Tape measure: For determining overall rise and run, as well as individual step dimensions.

Framing square: Helps lay out the stringer cuts with precision.

Level: To ensure your measurements and cuts are plumb and level. Cutting Tools

Once you’ve marked your stringers, you’ll need the following cutting tools:

Circular saw: The primary tool needed for making the long cuts on your stringers.

Handsaw, jigsaw, or reciprocating saw: For finishing cuts in corners where the circular saw can’t reach.

Drill: Useful for creating pilot holes and attaching hardware.

Pro Tip: When cutting stair stringers with a circular saw, don’t cut all the way into the corner where the tread meets the riser. Overcutting weakens the stringer at its most critical point. Instead, stop the circular saw just short of the intersection and finish the cut with a handsaw or reciprocating saw. This preserves the stringer’s structural integrity at every step. Safety Equipment

Safety should always be a priority when working with power tools. Don’t forget these essential safety items:

Safety glasses: Protect your eyes from sawdust and debris.

Hearing protection: Reduces noise exposure from power tools.

Calculating Stair Measurements for Cutting

Careful measurement is essential for cutting stair stringers. Here’s what to do. Determining Overall Rise and Run

The first step in calculating your stair measurements is to determine the overall rise and run:

First, measure the total vertical distance from the finished lower floor to the finished upper floor. This is your overall rise.

Then, measure the horizontal distance the stairs will cover. This is your overall run.

Pro Tip: When calculating your overall rise, account for the thickness of the finished flooring on both levels. As Norm Abram demonstrated on the Brookline House project, if the upper floor will have carpet (about 3/4 of an inch thick) and the lower floor will have a finish that’s about 1 1/2 inches above the subfloor, the difference of 3/4 of an inch must be subtracted from the rough-to-rough measurement. Failing to factor in finish floor thicknesses can throw off every riser in the staircase.

Calculating Riser Height and Tread Depth

Once you have the overall rise and run, you can calculate the individual riser height and tread depth by using the following steps:

Divide the overall rise by the desired number of steps to get the riser height. Most building codes allow a maximum riser height of 7 3/4 inches.

For tread depth, most codes require a minimum of 10 inches. This is a good standard to use, as deeper treads can weaken the stringer.

Pro Tip: Tom Silva explains why he keeps risers under 8 inches: “Risers higher than that leave you feeling like you’re climbing a ladder. And a tread more than 12 inches deep can force you to take a step and a half.” Building codes governing stair dimensions differ among states and even towns, so a check with your local building department will help you avoid grief later on.

For example, if your overall rise is 25 inches:

25 ÷ 7.75 = 3.23, which is rounded up to 4 risers

To find the individual riser height, divide the overall rise of 25 by the number of risers: 25 ÷ 4 = 6.25 inches per riser

Step-by-Step Guide To Cutting Stair Stringers

Now that you’ve gathered your tools and materials, it’s time to cut your stair stringers. Marking the First Stringer

Marking the first stringer accurately sets the stage for the remaining stringers. Here’s how to get it right: Lay a straight 2-by-12 on sawhorses.

Position your framing square with the rise dimension on the tongue and the run dimension on the blade, intersecting the edge of the 2-by12.

Clamp a scrap of wood to the underside of the square as a guide.

Mark the first cut with a pencil. The longer line is the tread cut (run), and the shorter line is the riser.

Move the square up the stringer, aligning the next tread cut with the top of the riser below, and mark the next tread and riser.

Continue until you’ve marked all tread and riser cuts. Adjusting for Tread Thickness

To account for tread thickness, follow these adjustments:

At the bottom of the stringer, shorten the bottom riser cut by the thickness of your tread material. Lay out this bottom cut by holding the square on the riser line and drawing a line parallel to the bottom tread.

At the top, the deck framing or fascia will act as the top riser, so make the final cut square down from the top tread. The final cut on the stringer is usually laid out and made square down from the top tread, depending on how you plan to tie the stringers into the deck framing.

Remember, there will always be one less tread than there are risers because the deck itself acts as the top step. Making the Cuts

Common Mistakes To Avoid When Cutting Stringers

Even experienced carpenters can make mistakes when cutting stair stringers. Here are some common pitfalls to avoid:

Overcutting corners: This weakens the stringer. Always stop saw cuts at the inside corners and finish with a handsaw.

Ignoring wood grain: Cut stringers so the grain runs parallel to the stair slope for maximum strength.

Failing to adjust for tread thickness: This can result in an uneven first step.

Using undersized lumber: Always use 2-by-12s for stringers to ensure adequate strength.

Neglecting to check for square: Use a framing square consistently to ensure accurate cuts.

Pro Tip: Use a master template, not chain copies. When cutting multiple stringers, always mark each one from the same original template rather than tracing one cut stringer to make the next. As Tom Silva demonstrated on a This Old House project, copying from copies compounds small errors: “I can’t cut one, use that as a template, cut another one, use that as a template, because the stairs will really be off.”

Advanced Techniques for Complex Staircases

While straight staircases are common, you may encounter situations that require more advanced stringer cutting techniques. Dealing with Winders

Winders are triangular steps used to change the direction of stairs, often in tight spaces. To cut stringers for winders:

Calculate the rise and run for each winder step individually.

Mark and cut each winder step on separate stringer sections.

Join the stringer sections carefully, ensuring proper alignment and support. Accommodating Landings

For stairs with landings, treat each flight of stairs as a separate unit with its own stringers. Ensure the landing is level and properly supported and attach the upper and lower stringers to the landing framing securely.

Remember, complex staircases often require additional support beyond standard stringers. Consider consulting a structural engineer for large or intricate projects.

Attaching Stair Stringers to the Structure

There are several methods you can use when attaching stringers, such as the following:

Direct attachment: Fasten the stringer directly to the floor joists or landing framing using lag screws or bolts.

Ledger board: Install a ledger board to the supporting structure, then attach the stringers to this board.

Joist hangers: Use specially designed joist hangers to support the top of the stringer. Using Stringer Connectors

Specialized hardware like the Simpson LSC Adjustable Stringer Connector can simplify attachment and provide additional support. These connectors allow for easy adjustment during installation and provide a strong, code-compliant connection.

Finishing Touches: Installing Treads and Risers

Once your stringers are cut and installed, it’s time to add the treads and risers. When adding treads and risers, use pressure-treated lumber or appropriate decking material for treads, especially for exterior stairs. Then, secure treads and risers with screws rather than nails for a more durable connection. Ensure treads are level and risers are plumb for a professional finish.

You may also want to consider adding a slight slope to exterior treads (about 1/8 inch per foot) to allow water runoff. If you encounter any squeaks after installation, you may need to fix squeaky stairs by reinforcing connections or adding shims.
