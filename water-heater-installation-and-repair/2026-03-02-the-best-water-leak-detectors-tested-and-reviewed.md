What to Consider When Choosing a Water Leak Detector Photo: Michelle Larson for Bob Vila

Water leak detectors offer a variety of features that impact their functionality. Understanding the differences between these features will help you choose the right water leak detector for your home. Detection Type, Coverage, and Location

When choosing a water leak detector, consider what kind of sensing method fits the location you want to protect, as detection type impacts coverage. Spot-style detectors with one or two sensors on their bodies are best for pinpointing leaks near toilets, sinks, washing machines, water heaters, dishwashers, and refrigerators, while cable-style detectors are better at providing extended coverage behind cabinets and appliances or along walls in basements, garages, or utility rooms. Putting a device in the upper areas of your home can help quickly identify leaks in the roof or ceiling. Alert Type and Smart Features

Alert type is an essential consideration because becoming aware of a leak as quickly as possible is the entire purpose of a detector. Basic detectors usually rely on very loud built-in alarms, typically with the same or greater decibel rating (85+ decibels) as smoke detectors (75+ decibels), and will stay active for several hours to a few days until manually silenced. Audible alarms work well if someone is home, while smart models that send app alerts, texts, or emails alert you to issues even if you’re away. Many models offer both types of alarms, and some also offer freeze and humidity notifications to help prevent frozen pipes and mold and mildew growth.

If you already use platforms like Alexa, Google Home, or Apple Home, smart–home-compatible leak detectors can be useful because some integrate directly with those ecosystems, while others rely on their own app and your home Wi‑Fi. Some models also require a separate hub or gateway to send alerts and support automations. Before buying, consider how the detector works to make setup and use easier and to avoid having to add extra equipment.

Of the many types of smart tools and appliances we’ve tested, smart water leak detectors are some of the most useful. Remote notifications can give you a major advantage if you travel often, own a second home, or want some peace of mind over less frequently accessed areas of your home, such as basements or crawl spaces. Installation and Power Source

Installation and power source also affect which detector is right for you. Battery-powered models are usually the easiest to install because they can go almost anywhere, while plug-in or wired units require a nearby outlet. Some detectors also use sensing cables or extension probes, which can be helpful if the water source is a distance away from the main unit.

Battery-powered leak detectors are the most common and are often seen as the most convenient since they don’t need a plug and will continue working even if a leak causes a circuit breaker to trip. Their main disadvantage is that batteries will eventually run out, which can leave the area unprotected if not caught in time. Fortunately, many battery-powered models last for years and have indicators that signal when battery life is low. Automatic Water Shutoff

Although uncommon, some water leak detectors include an automatic water shut-off system that turns off the supply as soon as it detects a leak. These “in-line” units are installed directly onto a water supply plumbing line and continually monitor a home’s water usage to detect leaks. Should a leak occur, they function much like a manual water shut-off valve by closing off the home’s water supply.

Leak detectors that incorporate automatic shutoff are typically expensive—usually several hundred dollars—but could potentially prevent thousands of dollars in water damage, making the extra cost worthwhile. These units are especially valuable for vacation homes and rental properties where the owner is frequently off-site and unable to monitor for leaks personally. Tips for Using a Water Leak Detector

To gain the most benefit from a water leak detector, start by identifying the sources of potential leaks. If you’re primarily concerned about plumbing fixtures, a small and compact leak detector will fit in the tight, confined spaces under sinks and next to toilets, washing machines, and dishwashers. If you’re concerned about flooding and need to cover a large floor area, consider a leak detector with a long, extendable sensor cable.

If the detector relies on a built-in siren for alerts, it needs to be loud enough to be heard throughout the house, regardless of where the detector is located. Generally speaking, this is relatively easy to accomplish inside the house, but some alarms might not be loud enough for use in a garage (especially a detached one) or an insulated basement. If you can’t hear the alarm from those locations, consider upgrading to a detector with a louder siren or one that can send alerts directly to a smartphone. Here are a few more tips for using a water leak detector:

Just like with a smoke alarm, routinely test a leak detector once every 1 to 2 months to verify it’s still working.

If a battery-operated leak detector doesn’t include a battery-life indicator, replace the batteries every 1 to 2 years to ensure they’re fully charged.

If a leak detector isn’t rated to withstand full water submersion, don’t place it in an area prone to flooding.

Water leaks caused by corroded pipes can also affect the color and taste of water. However, a number of other factors may be the cause, so it’s a good idea to check our article on common water problems. FAQs

Still have some lingering questions about water leak detectors? Consider the answers to the following frequently asked questions.

Q. Where do you put a water-leak detector?

You can put a water leak detector next to toilets, sinks, dishwashers, washing machines, and hot water heaters, as well as in garages, basements, or anywhere else prone to flooding. Putting a water leak detector in the attic can help identify problems with the roof caused by snow or storm damage.

Q. How do I find out where my water is leaking from?

If your water leak detector features a local alerting sensor and siren, the leak will be coming from where your detector is located and the siren was triggered. If you have a Wi-Fi–enabled or smart leak detector, your smartphone and other connected devices will help you find a water leak.

Q. How do you install a water-leak detector system?

Set a water leak detector directly on the ground (for waterproof units) or install it on the wall with double-sided tape, adhesive, or a similar mounting material.

Q. How long do water-leak detectors last?

The average lifespan of a water leak detector varies between manufacturers, but most detectors will last several years. However, this lifespan could be reduced by extreme temperatures or total water submersion.

Q. What is the most common water leak in the house?

According to First Alert, the most common water leaks “are often visible to the eye, stemming from places like underneath the kitchen sink, bathrooms or laundry rooms, burst plumbing pipes, and HVAC systems.” Q. Do water leak detectors really work?

Yes, water leak detectors work, especially when placed in the right location and maintained properly. They won’t fix the leak, but they can catch it early enough to help you reduce damage.

Q. Does home insurance cover leak detection?

No, homeowners’ insurance is more likely to cover repairing water damage from a leak than it is finding the issue. However, each insurer and policy is different, so it’s worth checking with your agent. Q. Is water leak detection worth it?

Yes, water leak detection is usually worth it because catching a leak early can prevent far more expensive water damage, mold, and repairs.
