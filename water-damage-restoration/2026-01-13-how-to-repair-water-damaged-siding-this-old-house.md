Signs of Water-Damaged Siding

Water-damaged siding typically shows up with soft or spongy areas with visible mold or mildew growth. Sometimes, you’ll also see siding panels warping or buckling, along with peeling paint or discoloration. In severe cases, you might even see water stains on your interior walls. Inspecting Siding for Water Damage

Conduct a thorough inspection of your siding, paying close attention to the following:

The bottom three courses of siding (about 2 feet up from the ground)

Wood-framed chimneys or chases not protected by roof overhangs Areas around windows and doors Joints between siding panels

Pro Tip: Joe Corsetto, president of the American Society of Home Inspectors (ASHI) and owner of Shelterworks, an inspection firm in New Jersey, says doors and windows are obvious places to start: “These will always take the brunt of abuse from rain and snow.” Look at sills and thresholds for cracks, and make sure caulking, weatherstripping, and glazing haven’t split or worn away.

Determining If Siding Replacement Is Necessary

Pro Tip: This Old House general contractor Tom Silva puts it simply: “Siding is a raincoat for your house. If you’ve got a hole in the raincoat, you’ve got problems.” If the damage has compromised your siding’s ability to shed water, it’s time to think about a full replacement rather than a patch.

In some cases, a repair may not be enough, and a replacement might be necessary. Consider replacement if the damage extends beyond the siding into the sheathing or framing or if it affects large sections of your siding. Additionally, if your siding is outdated, you might not be able to find an exact match. In that case, we recommend updating your home’s siding altogether. Tools and Materials To Repair Siding

To repair water-damaged siding, you’ll need these tools and materials: Pry bar Hammer Utility knife Circular saw Caulking gun Replacement siding panels Nails Building paper or housewrap Exterior-grade caulk Paint and primer (if necessary)

Pro Tip: This Old House general contractor Tom Silva uses only clear, all-heart, vertical-grain Western red cedar for replacement clapboards. As he explains, this top grade “looks better, holds paint better and is unlikely to warp or cup.” He also prefers factory-primed clapboards because priming protects all sides of the boards from moisture—including the often-forgotten back—which helps prevent paint from lifting and bubbling down the road. Step 1: Remove the Damaged Siding

[To Repair Water-Damaged Siding](https://to-repair-water-damaged-siding.swapstreet.shop/to-repair-water-damaged-siding/20260822.html)

Start at the top of the damaged area and work your way down. Use a pry bar to carefully remove the nails and separate the damaged siding from the wall. Cut out the damaged sections using a utility knife or circular saw.

Pro Tip: For wood clapboard siding, score the entire length of the damaged board from end to end with a utility knife, about halfway up from the bottom (butt) edge. Don’t slice all the way through the board, or you may cut the builder’s felt underneath. Then gently tap a small pry bar underneath the butt edge, next to a nail, and pull the bar toward you—don’t push it—so you don’t mar or crack the neighboring clapboards. Work the pry bar along the butt edge until the lower half breaks off. If this piece remains intact, save it as a template for sizing the replacement. Step 2: Address the Underlying Issues

Pro Tip: Tom Silva recommends tapping a hammer over the sheathing before making any repairs. “Listen for dull thuds that indicate rotten wood and loose boards,” he advises. Solid thumps help you locate studs. After replacing all the punky boards, shoot a few extra nails through the sheathing into the studs to tighten everything back up and restore structural strength.

Inspect the sheathing and framing for any water damage or rot. Replace any damaged structural elements. Apply a new layer of building paper or housewrap to protect against future water infiltration.

When applying housewrap over repaired sheathing, Silva notes it works “just like a Gore-Tex jacket”—stopping water and wind while still letting interior moisture escape. When cutting wrap around a window, leave enough extra material to tuck in behind the trim to ensure a watertight seal at these vulnerable transition points. Step 3: Install the New Siding

Pro Tip: Before sliding the new board into place, lay a thick bead of caulk on both ends of the opening where the new board will go. The bead should cover the end grain of the neighboring clapboard and the gap between its back side and the sheathing. This hidden layer of sealant is your best defense against water finding its way behind the repair.

Cut the new siding to fit the repaired area. Install the new siding panels, starting from the bottom and working up. Nail the siding securely, being careful not to overdrive the nails. Caulk any joints or seams to prevent water penetration. Paint the new siding to match the existing color, if necessary. Preventing Future Water Damage

Proper installation and regular maintenance are key factors to keep your siding in good condition and prevent further issues.

Pro Tip: This Old House General Contractor Tom Silva warns that siding installed too close to a roof line is a major culprit: “When the gap is less than an inch, wood siding wicks up moisture and takes longer to dry out, creating an ideal environment for mold, algae, and rot.” He recommends leaving at least a 2-inch gap between roofing and the siding’s bottom edge to minimize prolonged moisture exposure. Proper Siding Installation Techniques

Install your siding correctly to make sure it doesn’t get damaged again. Keep proper clearance between your siding and the ground (at least 6 inches for most materials), and add in the correct flashing around windows, doors, and other openings.

When To Call a Professional to Repair Water-Damaged Siding

While many siding repairs can be DIY projects, there are times when it’s best to call in a professional. Knowing when to seek expert help can save you time, effort, and potentially further damage to your home. Call a professional in these circumstances:

If the damage extends into the structural elements of your home, such as the frame When dealing with large areas of damage

If you’re unsure about the extent of the damage or proper repair techniques

When working with specialized siding materials that require expert handling

Professional contractors have the experience and tools to assess and repair water damage better than the average homeowner. Our Conclusion
